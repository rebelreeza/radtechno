Initial Assessment

My Read From the Outside

What the outside view suggests before I've had eyes on the actual systems. Both where the risk lives and where the real leverage is.

Where the Risk Lives
ERP Health

Odoo as source of truth

Odoo is the operational backbone. The question I need to answer early is how much it's actually trusted vs. worked around. The gap between system-of-record and how teams actually operate is where most operational friction lives.

Shopify Sync

A high-surface, high-frequency integration

Inventory counts, order states, and fulfillment events have to stay in sync across ~70 stores and Odoo. Understanding where that integration is reliable and where it isn't is a week-one priority.

Institutional Knowledge

Context behind the configuration

The decisions behind how Odoo is set up carry real weight. Understanding what was intentional, what was inherited, and what was expedient determines what can safely change and what has to be worked around.

Drop Coordination

Launch execution overhead

Coordinating a drop across inventory, Shopify, fulfillment, and creator comms is high-touch. At ~70 stores the coordination cost per launch compounds. The question is how much of that is structural vs. improvised.

Where the Leverage Is
AI Enablement

Embedded, not bolted on

The opportunity isn't adopting AI tools. It's embedding AI into the decisions teams are already making: ops triage, inventory calls, creative briefs, launch coordination. That's a different outcome from rolling out subscriptions, and a harder one to replicate once it's working.

Automation Consolidation

From glue code to real infrastructure

An audit of existing automation will surface what's load-bearing, what's redundant, and where to consolidate. At this scale, n8n self-hosted starts to make more economic and operational sense than per-task pricing, and typically frees up meaningful SaaS spend in the process.

Creator Intelligence

Purchase data by fanbase, not brand

Each store produces distinct purchase behavior tied to a specific creator's audience. Aggregated across ~70, that's a merchandising and product intelligence layer that compounds with every drop and is structurally hard for a brand-first competitor to replicate.

Portfolio Visibility

One view across all stores

Odoo, Shopify, Airtable, and PipeDrive are producing data independently. Connecting them into a single pipeline gives leadership a live view of inventory, sell-through, and creator performance. Achievable in the first 90 days once the data foundation is clean.

The Real Risk

The business is moving faster than the systems

Creators move at the speed of social media. A drop that goes viral on a Tuesday needs the ops infrastructure to handle it by Wednesday. Right now, the reliability of that infrastructure is unclear. At ~70 stores, a systemic failure isn't a one-store problem.

First 90 Days

Where I'm Focusing

Stabilize the foundation fast, build leverage into it, then compound. The sequencing is intentional. Later phases depend on earlier ones being solid.

Days 1 – 30 · Phase 1
Orient: Understand before acting

The output of month one is a ranked list of what's actually broken, not a change log. I won't fix anything that isn't causing live operational damage.

  • Structured listening sessions with every team: ops, finance, merch, creative, partnerships. Not just systems questions: what's pulling you away from the thing you're actually here to do? What's broken that everyone's learned to live with? What's working that should be protected? If you had one thing fixed tomorrow, what is it?
  • Sit with ops, finance, and merch specifically on Odoo. Ask "where do you go when you can't trust it?" and map every workaround in use
  • Pull a sample of orders and trace them end-to-end through Shopify and Odoo to find where states diverge
  • Map every manual reconciliation happening at month-end
  • Full Shopify audit: plan types, app overlap, store structure consistency, sync behavior
  • SaaS and vendor audit to identify consolidation and cost reduction opportunities
  • Ship one high-visibility AI automation early to prove the pattern, not to impress, to establish the template

Months 2 – 3 · Phase 2
Stabilize: Make Odoo trustworthy

The foundational work that later phases depend on. The measure of success is a measurable reduction in data errors and the manual effort spent correcting them.

  • Fix the Shopify sync: monitoring, alerting, and conflict resolution documented
  • Clean the data sources that are producing unreliable reports (not the reports themselves)
  • Automate the 3 most painful manual handoffs identified in Phase 1
  • Stand up the data pipeline to BigQuery so Phase 3 has clean data to work with

Months 4 – 6 · Phase 3
Build: Automate, surface intelligence, ship dashboards

Clean data is only useful if something is built on top of it. This phase turns the foundation into visible, operational output.

  • Live portfolio dashboard: inventory, sell-through, and margin across all stores in one view
  • RAG layer on campaign history, creator briefs, and product data, AI outputs informed by what actually worked, not generic responses
  • Launch readiness agent: checks inventory, Shopify, and fulfillment before each drop and flags gaps automatically
  • Cross-store audience profiles encoded as structured data, each store's price sensitivity, category affinity, and sell-through patterns. Sets up Phase 4.
  • Non-obvious handoff automations: inventory pre-positioning ahead of drops, creator sign-to-ops handoff, sell-through velocity alerts

By this point

The operational pain that dominated month one should be structurally solved, not managed. Specifically:

  • No more manual month-end reconciliations
  • Sync failures caught automatically, not discovered after a drop
  • Launch coordination is a checklist the system runs, not a Slack thread
  • Leadership has a live view of portfolio performance without pulling exports
  • Teams are getting AI-assisted outputs in the tools they already use
Months 7 – 9 · Phase 4
Compound: Where the data flywheel kicks in

This is what the earlier phases were built toward. The infrastructure is clean, the audience profiles exist, the AI layer has context. Now it compounds.

  • Cross-store campaign intelligence: when a drop overperforms, automatically identify stores with the most similar audience profile and generate tailored briefs. Gets more accurate with every launch.
  • Predictive drop timing and inventory positioning: sell-through patterns across all stores feed recommendations on when to launch and how much to hold, per creator and category
  • Purchasing consolidation: stores sharing manufacturers surface automatically, enabling negotiation as a single buyer
  • Proprietary playbook library: every successful drop encodes its performance drivers. An institutional intelligence layer that compounds with time and is structurally hard to replicate from scratch

Bigger Picture

Where This Leads

The 6–9 month work is about stabilizing the foundation. But the reason to build it carefully is what it unlocks. These are the outcomes that justify the investment. None of them are possible without clean data and reliable infrastructure underneath.

Flagship Opportunity

Cross-Store Campaign Pattern Matching

WJ manages ~70 creator stores, more launch data than any individual creator will accumulate on their own. After every successful drop, leverage agentic AI to extract what drove performance: audience trust index, product category, price band, scarcity mechanics, sell-through velocity. When Store A has a breakout launch, the system could identify the 4–5 stores with the most similar fingerprint and generate tailored campaign briefs for each. Over time this could become a proprietary playbook library that compounds with every new launch, the kind of institutional intelligence that's hard to replicate without the same data history.

New Revenue Line

Creator-Owned Apps

Hank Green's Focus Friend hit #1 on the App Store in 2025 with a tiny team. The distribution came entirely from his audience, not paid acquisition. WJ's creator roster has the same distribution potential, and most creators have no product or tech expertise to act on it. That gap could be a material opportunity enabled by a tech org with app-building capability and product judgment.

Operations

Launch Command Center

Coordinating a drop across inventory, fulfillment, Shopify, and creator comms is typically a manual high-stress event. With the right automation layer, that could look more like a pre-staged, orchestrated workflow: inventory positioned ahead of time, Launchpad queued, notifications handled automatically.

Finance

Real-Time Portfolio View

With clean Odoo data flowing into BigQuery, a live portfolio view across all ~70 stores (revenue, inventory, margin) becomes achievable without anyone manually pulling and reconciling exports.

Working Together

How We'll Work

Weekly sync, short by default

30 minutes, structured: what shipped, what's blocked, what's coming. I'll send a brief ahead of time so we're not using the meeting to catch up.

Decisions to you, not recommendations

When I need a call from you, I'll bring a clear recommendation with the tradeoffs laid out, not a list of options. Your time is for deciding, not deliberating.

No surprises

If something breaks or I find something unexpected, you hear it from me first, before it surfaces in a meeting or from a team member. Early signal, not managed perception.

You're a technical partner in this

I'll keep you close enough to the technology that you can have an informed opinion, not buried in it or kept at arm's length. That makes the whole thing go faster.

VP of Brands Partnership

The VP of Brands role is tackling the same set of problems from the brand side: make launches less heroic, build repeatable playbooks, give the organization better visibility, reduce cross-functional friction. These two roles have to be coordinated from the start, not running in parallel and hoping things align.

  • Launch readiness: Brand is focused on better launch planning; Tech is building the infrastructure that makes each drop pre-staged instead of improvised. The process and the system need to be designed together or one will undermine the other.
  • Campaign data: The attribution discipline planned for Phase 3 (UTM tagging, Shopify Flow, consistent tracking across ~70 stores) directly serves Brand's need to understand what's actually driving sell-through. Brand should define the questions; Tech builds the pipeline to answer them.
  • Portfolio visibility: Brand needs to run creator accounts like a GM and wants a live view of revenue, inventory, and margin across the portfolio. The cross-store dashboard should be built to answer their actual questions, not ones I guessed at.
  • Playbook operationalization: Brand wants repeatable playbooks. Tech can encode those into systems: Shopify flows, Asana templates, automated handoffs. The process has to come first or the system won't match how Brand actually works.

A few places where lines could get unclear: who owns Shopify platform decisions vs. store-level business outcomes, who defines what the data products should answer vs. who builds them, and who leads process design vs. system implementation. Worth getting ahead of that before both roles are moving fast in the same space.

The Approach

How I Think About This Work

Technical execution is table stakes. What makes the difference here is combining strategic judgment, cross-functional leadership, and hands-on delivery, all at the same time, not in sequence.

Strategic product thinking

I've led product from zero to shipped across multiple domains, not just feature delivery but the harder work of deciding what to build, what to buy, what to kill, and what sequencing actually makes business sense. That judgment is what keeps a tech roadmap from becoming a list of interesting projects.

Cross-functional leadership

Tech decisions at WJ touch ops, finance, creative, and creator relationships simultaneously. I'm comfortable owning the room in those conversations, not as the person who explains the tech, but as a peer who understands the tradeoffs each function is navigating and can drive alignment across them.

Vendor accountability and negotiation

ERP implementations, Shopify connectors, SaaS contracts: these relationships have real commercial leverage that often goes unused. I know how to hold vendors to commitments, escalate when support is falling short, and negotiate from an informed position rather than just accepting the default.

I'm a doer

I don't hand off and wait. When something needs to move fast, I'll be in the configuration, in the code, or in the vendor call myself. The strategic thinking and the rolling-up-sleeves are not separate modes. They run in parallel, which is what this stage of WJ needs.

Honest Gaps

What I Don't Know Yet

Transparency up front is more useful than confidence that turns out to be wrong. These are the areas where I'm starting from scratch and will need context, patience, or both.

Creator commerce is a new domain

I know how to build systems that make creator businesses run. I don't yet know WJ's specific creator relationships, what makes each one tick, or the commercial dynamics of how these deals are structured. That context will build, but not before day one.

Everything here is inference

I haven't seen the real state of Odoo, how the Shopify stores are actually configured, or what's genuinely living in Airtable. Some of what's in this document will turn out to be wrong. Phase 1 is largely about finding out where before it causes problems.

No read on the people yet

I don't know who the strong operators are, where the institutional knowledge lives, or what the internal dynamics look like. Getting that right takes real time and I won't shortcut it.

WJ's commercial model is still opaque

The economics of how a creator partnership works at WJ: margin structure, deal terms, what a healthy relationship looks like for both sides, is something I need to learn from the inside. My instincts will sharpen once I understand where the money actually comes and goes.
